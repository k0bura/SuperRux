Three supplies, one ground

The wiring mistake that costs you a day isn't a swapped signal pin — it's the power topology. Logic and motors run from separate supplies that must share a ground, and VM must never touch VCC.

Do not

Never tie VM to VCC, and never run the motors from the ESP32's 3V3 pin — not even though the motors are 3 V rated. The question is current, not voltage. That pin is the output of the devkit's little AMS1117 regulator, already feeding the ESP32's own ~250 mA WiFi bursts, off a 500 mA USB budget. One of these gearmotors pulls several hundred mA stalled.

And phase 1 stalls them deliberately — that is what homing into a hard stop is. Three axes, ~900 ms each, at the worst-case draw. The regulator browns out, the ESP32's brownout detector fires, and it resets mid-home.

Motor supply — these are 3 V motors

Aim for 3.7–4.5 V on VM: a single LiPo cell, or 3 × AA. The TB6612 drops roughly 0.3–0.5 V across the bridge, so 3 V in would leave the motor short; 3.7 V in lands about 3.3 V at the motor, and duty does the rest of the trimming. Do not use the bear's original 6 V — that fed the whole toy, and the servo amplifier never put the full rail across a motor.

Signal wiring, pin to pin

Both drivers take their logic pins in the same physical order, so if you lay them out as shown the runs stay parallel and nothing crosses. Motor supply is in Figure 1 — this is everything else.

Pin numbers, not pin positions

Hosyond ships both 30-pin and 38-pin ESP32 boards and the physical pin order differs between them. Every one of them silkscreens the GPIO number next to the hole — find D25, D33 and so on by reading the board, not by counting from a corner.

Pull the standby pins down

Between reset and the first line of setup(), GPIO 21 and GPIO 19 float — and a floating STBY can kick a motor on every boot. Put a 10 kΩ from each to ground so the drivers stay disabled until firmware deliberately enables them.

Five wires per motor — use two, save three

Each plug carries 2 motor wires (the pair with the suppression capacitors across them) and 3 potentiometer wires going to the plastic body behind the gearbox. Only the motor pair goes to the driver. This matches the "controllerless 5-wire servo" in the original design: the motor and its position sensor sat in the head, and the servo amplifier lived on the main board.

Tell them apart in thirty seconds: the motor pair reads 2–30 Ω and twitches on a brief touch of a cell. On the pot, the two outer wires read a fixed value — 5 k, 10 k, whatever it is — that does not change as you turn the mechanism by hand, while each outer-to-wiper reading sweeps smoothly and the two always sum to that total.

The pots are good — wire them

Measured on the eyes axis: a 5 kΩ pot whose wiper sweeps 0.45 k → 3.92 k smoothly, with no dead spots. That is absolute position feedback, and it is the one thing PLAN.md assumed the bear did not have. See Potentiometer feedback below.

Figure 3

Potentiometer feedback

Each pot is a plain voltage divider: both ends across 3.3 V and ground, wiper to an analog input. Nothing you have already wired moves — these land on pins that are input-only and free.

3.3 V only

The pot ends go to 3V3 and GND — never 5 V, never VM. The wiper feeds an ADC input directly, and anything above 3.3 V on that pin damages it. Which end takes 3V3 is arbitrary; it only decides whether "up" reads high or low, and the firmware calibrates either way.

Why these pins

ADC2 is unusable while WiFi is running, so pot wipers must land on ADC1 — GPIO 32–39. GPIO 34, 36 and 39 are also input-only, which is perfect for an analog input and costs no output pin. The I2S mic data line, once penciled in for 34/35, moves to GPIO 15 when the audio phase arrives.

Last step

Before you power on

  1. Meter continuity between the two grounds. ESP32 GND to each driver's GND should read a short. This is the single most common cause of "the motors do nothing".
  2. Meter VM against VCC. They must not be connected. If they beep, stop and find the bridge.
  3. Check the electrolytic's polarity. The stripe is the negative leg and goes to ground. Backwards, it vents.
  4. Power the motor pack first, USB second, and watch for the ESP32 resetting when a motor starts — that's the brownout the bulk cap exists to prevent.
  5. Expect a motor to run backwards. Swap that motor's two output leads, or flip offsetA / offsetB / offsetC in Calibrate.ino from 1 to -1. The mouth needs upper and lower opposed, so at least one of them very likely needs it.
  6. Then flash and open the console at 115200 and type help. It stall-homes all three axes on boot, so keep a hand near the pack.
