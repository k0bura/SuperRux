Teddy Ruxpin · how the mouth is driven

No letters, only loudness

The bear does not know what it is saying. Nothing in the pipeline detects a letter, a phoneme, or a sound. The jaw is driven by one number — how loud the audio is right now — and the surprising part is that this is not a shortcut. The mechanism is too slow to render anything finer.

The thing you are picturing

There is no letter-matching step

It is natural to assume a mouth that moves with speech must be recognising speech: spotting an oo and rounding, spotting an m and closing. Real lip-sync animation does work that way, with a table mapping phonemes to mouth shapes.

This does none of that. There is no speech recognition, no phoneme table, no viseme mapping, and no text involved at any point — the jaw would behave identically if you fed it a cello. The entire input is a loudness measurement taken 50 times a second.

What actually happens

Four steps, no cleverness

01

Chop the audio into 20 ms frames

Each frame gets one number: its RMS level, converted to decibels. A frame is short enough to catch a syllable, long enough to be stable.

02

Gate the silence

Anything more than 25 dB below the voice's reference level counts as silence and forces the mouth fully shut. This closure between phrases does more visible work than any other single step.

03

Smooth it

A one-pole filter, opening faster than it closes. Mouths snap open and relax shut, and the asymmetry is most of what separates speech from a flapping hinge.

04

Map to a jaw percentage

Scaled into the lower half of the jaw's travel — commanding the full range asks for motion the motor cannot deliver, and the loop just saturates. The bear closes its own position loop from there, reading a potentiometer on each motor's output shaft.

Why that is enough

The mechanism is a 2 Hz filter

Drive the jaw with a sine wave and sweep the rate, and it behaves like a textbook low-pass filter. At 1 Hz it reaches everything it was asked for. By 5 Hz it manages a seventh of it.

Speech structureRateRendered
Phrasing and prosody1–3 Hz42–105%
Syllables3–8 Hz14–42%
Phonemes — individual sounds10–15 Hz~5%

This is the whole argument. A perfect phoneme-accurate animation would be filtered away to about 5% of its intended motion. The gearmotor physically cannot move that fast, so the effort would buy almost no visible difference. Loudness is not an approximation of the right answer here — at the rates this mechanism can actually render, it is most of the way to it.

The cases where loudness genuinely misleads are real but fast. An m is voiced and loud with the mouth shut; an s is quiet with the mouth open. Both are phoneme-rate events, and both get smoothed into their neighbours before the jaw could have shown them.

The 1985 cassette

The tape did the opposite

The original tapes are stereo, and the two channels have nothing to do with each other — measured correlation between them is 0.02. The left channel is the story. The right channel is a digital control track: a framed pulse signal repeating at 58 Hz, carrying six discrete data fields per frame.

Here is the part that answers your question. That track is not an envelope. Nothing in it correlates with how loud the voice is — across a whole 14-minute story, no field reaches even a weak correlation with the audio in any 10-second window. Its values are discrete, not continuous: one field resolves into three cleanly separated levels with literally nothing in the gaps between them.

So the tape was not deriving motion from sound. It was replaying motion somebody authored, stored alongside the sound and synchronised to it only because they sat on the same piece of tape.

The trade is honest. Hand-authored animation can do things loudness cannot — hold a shape through a pause, close on an m, act. What it cannot do is respond to a sentence nobody wrote in advance, which is the entire point of this build.

Two motors, not one

The upper jaw moves less

The mouth has two motors, upper and lower, and the original drove them separately. That detail matters more than it sounds: a mouth whose halves move as one reads as a hinge opening, not as a face speaking. So the upper jaw gets the same envelope scaled down, and each half closes its own position loop against its own potentiometer.

The scaling is not linear, because at speech rates both halves are slew-limited — a smaller command does not produce a proportionally smaller movement. Commanding 40% yields about 65% visually, so the rule of thumb is to command roughly half the split you want to see.

That last figure is why the jaw is commanded ahead of the sound. Between the motor's dead time and the loop's settling, the mouth arrives about an eighth of a second after it is told to move, so the envelope is sent early by exactly that much.

The eyes

Driven by rhythm, not level

The eyes deliberately ignore loudness. Amplitude-driven eyes look wrong — they read as a meter, not a face. Instead blinks are placed on phrase structure: in the gaps where the mouth is already shut, topped up to a natural idle rate when the speech does not supply enough pauses.

Because a blink ramp is slow — about 340 ms each way — it sits comfortably inside the mechanism's bandwidth, and the difference shows:

The eyes track almost perfectly. That is not a better algorithm — it is the same constraint seen from the other side. Commands inside the bandwidth get rendered faithfully; the jaw's 0.878 is speech-rate detail the motor cannot follow. Eye animation needs no cleverness to look right, and the jaw will always be an approximation.

Still unknown

What the six fields mean

The control track's framing is decoded; its payload is not. Six discrete fields, three motors, and no mapping between them yet. Three hypotheses have been tested and rejected: that a field is a position proportional to loudness, that the intervals are a simple pulse count, and that it is biphase-mark encoding like SMPTE timecode.

Cracking it would not change how the bear speaks. It would tell us what the original animators thought the right answer was — a ground truth to check the envelope against, from people who solved this problem once already, on the same three motors.
